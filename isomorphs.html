Isomorphs

Two stretches of ciphertext are isomorphic when they have the same pattern of repeats (Lymm's wiki has a page on them): wherever one repeats a symbol, the other repeats a symbol in the same places. The symbols themselves can be completely different.

E1 30–48  21 19  0 40 51 65 26 14 21 70 47 44 48 42 19 48 13 47 19
E2 35–53  40 49 78  3 16 56 19 47 40 80  6 13 64 29 49 64 63  6 49
           A  B  .  .  .  .  .  .  A  .  C  .  D  .  B  D  .  C  B

Orange marks a symbol that occurs more than once in its row, and the letters under the rows name the repeats: both rows have the pattern A B . . . . . . A and so on. The E2 stretch begins five cells later: its shift is +5. The two stretches are called copies of each other. The arcs join each repeat to its next occurrence; hovering over a cell highlights its column and its repeats.

The usual explanation is that both rows are the same text, enciphered from two different states of the key (the deck model shows how that produces exactly this). Then the pair gives a copy map, a rule for turning a symbol of the first row into the symbol at the same place in the second: here 21 becomes 40, 19 becomes 49, 0 becomes 78. Each such pairing is a link. A link seen at two or more cells, like 21 → 40, is repeat-supported; a link seen once, like 0 → 78, rests on a single cell.

That the two rows are the same text is a hypothesis, in two strengths. R: the letters are equal where symbols repeat (the lettered cells). W: they are equal at every cell of the span. R is the safer one: a pattern of repeats that lines up is hard to get by chance, while a cell whose symbol occurs once would look the same whether or not the text matched there.

The list

A pair is listed if it has at least three repeats, or two repeats over at least 12 cells. Pairs at the same shift in the same two messages are merged into one span. Stretches copied symbol for symbol, like the shared openings, are left out. That gives 30 pairs. Cell numbers count from 0 in each message. A shift of +5 means the second copy starts five cells later in its message than the first does in its own.

Twelve of these are used for the tests of rotations, reflections and affine shuffles on the exclusions page: the pairs with four or more repeats at a shift other than 0, counting the two halves of E4/E5 separately. All twelve lie inside the first or the last group of messages.

Every other pair stays inside one of the three groups of messages: E1, W1, E2; W2, E3, W3; E4, W4, E5. The exceptions involve E3. Its passage from cell 64 reappears in all three of E4, W4 and E5 (used below), and a shorter one from cell 77 does too, so E3 links the middle group to the last. W1 and E3 is the only pair between the first group and the others. In shuffled copies of the messages, a match this strong somewhere between those groups turned up 3 times in 46, so it may be chance. Nothing on this site depends on it.

One fixed shuffle cannot fit these three copies

In the deck model each letter reads the card at its own position and the deck is then shuffled. Suppose there is only one shuffle, the same after every letter. Assume fixed read positions, the same starting deck for all messages, and cell 0 outside encryption. Express the shuffle on symbols as Q (definition). Equal letters at a shift of d must then be related by Q applied d times. Three isomorphs give three facts, each seen at two cells.

From W1 to E2 the shift is 10, and 68 becomes 41:

The first says ten shuffles take 68 to 41. The other two say one shuffle takes 68 to 30 and nine more take 30 to 26. Both cannot be true, so there are at least two shuffles: K ≥ 2. Needs R. Check the certificate.

Q10(68) = 41                              W1 → E2
Q10(68) = Q9(Q(68)) = Q9(30) = 26          E4 → E5, E3 → E4
41 ≠ 26, so K ≥ 2

The order of two copies matters

One passage of E3 appears again in E5, ten cells later, and in W4, twelve cells later:

E3 64–88   58  9 70  1 58 56 63 68 25 79  7 20 19 64  2 66 73 30 71 16 12 30 65 37 20
E5 74–98   25 45 42 80 25 12 22 76 20 51 62 21 40  9 41 10 44 73  8 33 70 73  6 31 21
W4 76–100  66 47 53 34 66 23 73 31 54 38 77 67 11 63 79  6 22 21 51 69 74 21  5 17 67
            A  .  .  .  A  .  .  .  .  .  .  B  .  .  .  .  .  C  .  .  .  C  .  .  B

Read each copy as a rule for turning an E3 symbol into a new one: call the E5 rule A and the W4 rule B. At cells 64 and 68, A turns 58 into 25 and B turns 58 into 66. Now apply both, in each order. A then B: 58 becomes 25, and B turns 25 (cell 72) into 54. B then A: 58 becomes 66, and A turns 66 (cell 79) into 10. The two orders give 54 and 10.

B(A(58)) = B(25) = 54
A(B(58)) = A(66) = 10            AB ≠ BA

Two rules commute when applying them in either order gives the same result, and rotations of a ring do. So A and B are not both rotations of one ring, however the ring is labelled. That excludes any cipher whose copy maps must be rotations: cyclic deck walks and additive autokeys. Needs W: 25 and 66 each appear only once in E3's passage. Check the maps.

Where a copy breaks

E5 copies E4 one cell later from cell 51 to cell 97, but not with a single rule. Cells 51 to 67 are isomorphic:

E4 51–67  47 45 58 68 69 79 25 38 45 73 47 68 50 34 45 78 26
E5 52–68   7 34 32 30 31 19 26  8 34 46  7 30 71 55 34 75 54
           A  B  .  C  .  .  .  .  B  .  A  C  .  .  B  .  .

and so are cells 68 to 97:

E4 68–97  79 57  4 56 22 60 18 75 43 60 59 67 63 42 49 33 40 65 79 77  7  3 26 62 31 78 26 57 69 40
E5 69–98   9  6 60  5 23 25 45 42 80 25 12 22 76 20 51 62 21 40  9 41 10 44 73  8 33 70 73  6 31 21

but the two halves disagree. Four E4 symbols occur in both. In the first half 26 becomes 54, 78 becomes 75 and 79 becomes 19. In the second, 26 becomes 73, 78 becomes 70 and 79 becomes 9. Only 69 keeps its partner, 31.

F (cells 51–67):  26 → 54   78 → 75   79 → 19   69 → 31
G (cells 68–97):  26 → 73   78 → 70   79 → 9    69 → 31

If each half is shared text, the change at the break, G F−1, takes 54, 75 and 19 to 73, 70 and 9. Symbol 8 moves too: it is the partner of 38 in the first half and of 62 in the second. That makes at least seven symbols moved, and at least five even if the cell where the break happens is left out of both halves. A difference in the text near the break that sends the two messages through different shuffles would do this; the cells do not say which letters or which shuffles. Needs W: each mapping in the first half is seen once.

The table counts links that pair two different symbols, once each however many pairs of copies show them. The last column is the largest set of symbols joined to each other by chains of links. The link ledger records the supporting cell numbers for every mapping. It contains 451 mappings across 31 spans, counting the E4/E5 halves separately: 114 repeat-supported, 337 seen only once. Two symbols are connected if a chain of links joins them, in either direction.

The K ≥ 2 certificate uses only repeat-supported mappings. The non-commuting pair, the six-symbol break and the affine exclusion use cells seen once and need W. Symbol 27 is outside every link: it occurs twice in the bodies, at W4 35 and E5 33, and no copy maps it to a different symbol. So the links do not show that the shuffles can carry any symbol to any other, and they do not identify the group the shuffles generate.
