Isomorphs

Two stretches of ciphertext are isomorphic when they have the same pattern of repeats (Lymm's wiki has a page on them): wherever one repeats a symbol, the other repeats a symbol in the same places. The symbols themselves can be completely different.

E1 30–48  21 19  0 40 51 65 26 14 21 70 47 44 48 42 19 48 13 47 19
E2 35–53  40 49 78  3 16 56 19 47 40 80  6 13 64 29 49 64 63  6 49
           A  B  .  .  .  .  .  .  A  .  C  .  D  .  B  D  .  C  B

The E2 stretch begins five cells later: its shift is +5. The pattern is observable; whether these are copies of the same plaintext is a separate hypothesis. A row with no repeats supplies no repeated-symbol evidence.

R assumes corresponding plaintext letters are equal at repeat-supported positions; W extends that to every aligned cell of the selected span. The arguments below also specify the cipher mechanism they constrain.

The list

A pair is listed if it has at least three repeats, or two repeats over at least 12 cells. Pairs at the same shift in the same two messages are merged into one span. Stretches copied symbol for symbol, like the shared openings, are left out. That gives 30 pairs. Cell numbers count from 0 in each message.

One fixed shuffle cannot fit these three copies

Assume fixed read positions, one shuffle after every letter, and the same starting deck for all messages, with cell 0 outside encryption. Express the shuffle on symbols as Q (definition). Equal letters at a shift of d must then be related by Q applied d times. Three isomorphs give three facts, each seen at two cells.

From W1 to E2 the shift is 10, and 68 becomes 41:

W1 70–77  68 46 17 36 13 17 21 68
E2 80–87  41 72 57 20 23 57 65 41
           A  .  B  .  .  B  .  A

From E4 to E5 the shift is 1, and 68 becomes 30:

E4 54–62  68 69 79 25 38 45 73 47 68
E5 55–63  30 31 19 26  8 34 46  7 30
           A  .  .  .  .  .  .  .  A

From E3 to E4 the shift is 9, and 30 becomes 26:

E3 81–85  30 71 16 12 30
E4 90–94  26 62 31 78 26
           A  .  .  .  A

The first says ten shuffles take 68 to 41. The other two say one shuffle takes 68 to 30 and nine more take 30 to 26. Both cannot be true: this shared-start single-shuffle model fails. Within the class-shuffle family, K ≥ 2. Needs R, not whole-passage equality. Check the certificate.

Q10(68) = 41                              W1 → E2
Q10(68) = Q9(Q(68)) = Q9(30) = 26          E4 → E5, E3 → E4
41 ≠ 26, so K ≥ 2

The order of two copies matters

One passage of E3 appears again in E5, ten cells later, and in W4, twelve cells later:

E3 64–88   58  9 70  1 58 56 63 68 25 79  7 20 19 64  2 66 73 30 71 16 12 30 65 37 20
E5 74–98   25 45 42 80 25 12 22 76 20 51 62 21 40  9 41 10 44 73  8 33 70 73  6 31 21
W4 76–100  66 47 53 34 66 23 73 31 54 38 77 67 11 63 79  6 22 21 51 69 74 21  5 17 67
            A  .  .  .  A  .  .  .  .  .  .  B  .  .  .  .  .  C  .  .  .  C  .  .  B

Read each copy as a rule for turning an E3 symbol into a new one: call the E5 rule A and the W4 rule B. At cells 64 and 68, A turns 58 into 25 and B turns 58 into 66. Now apply both, in each order. A then B: 58 becomes 25, and B turns 25 (cell 72) into 54. B then A: 58 becomes 66, and A turns 66 (cell 79) into 10. The two orders give 54 and 10.

B(A(58)) = B(25) = 54
A(B(58)) = A(66) = 10            AB ≠ BA

Rotations of a fixed ring commute: their order makes no difference. These maps therefore cannot both be rotations under any one relabelling of that ring. This excludes cyclic deck walks and other mechanisms requiring each passage-copy map to be one fixed rotation. It is not a blanket proof against every position-dependent scheme called Vigenère or a rotor cipher: that rotation property must first be established for the proposed mechanism. Needs W: 25 and 66 each appear only once in E3's passage. Check the maps.

Where a copy breaks

E5 copies E4 one cell later from cell 51 to cell 97, but not with a single rule. Cells 51 to 67 are isomorphic:

E5 52–68   7 34 32 30 31 19 26  8 34 46  7 30 71 55 34 75 54
           A  B  .  C  .  .  .  .  B  .  A  C  .  .  B  .  .

and so are cells 68 to 97:

E4 68–97  79 57  4 56 22 60 18 75 43 60 59 67 63 42 49 33 40 65 79 77  7  3 26 62 31 78 26 57 69 40
E5 69–98   9  6 60  5 23 25 45 42 80 25 12 22 76 20 51 62 21 40  9 41 10 44 73  8 33 70 73  6 31 21
           A  B  .  .  .  C  .  .  .  C  .  .  .  .  .  .  D  .  A  .  .  .  E  .  .  .  E  B  .  D

but the two halves disagree. Four E4 symbols occur in both. In the first half 26 becomes 54, 78 becomes 75 and 79 becomes 19. In the second, 26 becomes 73, 78 becomes 70 and 79 becomes 9. Only 69 keeps its partner, 31.

F (cells 51–67):  26 → 54   78 → 75   79 → 19   69 → 31
G (cells 68–97):  26 → 73   78 → 70   79 → 9    69 → 31

If each half represents shared plaintext under a fixed permutation map, the relative map G F−1 moves 54, 75 and 19 to 73, 70 and 9. All six distinct symbols must move. A plaintext edit changing the shuffle history could explain the break, but these cells do not identify a particular edit or individual shuffle. Needs W for each half: the before-break mappings occur only once.
