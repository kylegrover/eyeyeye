Noita eye messages

Noita is a game by Nolla Games. Beyond the edges of its main world lie parallel worlds, and in the first ones to the east and west there are nine walls covered in eye glyphs: five in the east, called E1 to E5, and four in the west, W1 to W4. Each eye looks in one of five directions. Three eyes make one symbol, a number from 0 to 82. The nine messages hold 1,036 symbols, and nobody has read them.

Above, the first six symbols of E1: under each triangle of three eyes are its three digits and the symbol they make. Hover over a triangle to number its eyes in reading order. Basics & data has the reading rule and all nine messages as numbers.

These pages collect what can be proved about the cipher, which keys have been searched, and what the repeats say about the text. Each result names the assumption it needs.

The messages repeat each other in three ways

A cell is a place in a message, counted from 0. Cell 0 is different in all nine messages. After it, E1, W1 and E2 hold the same 24 symbols, and E4, W4 and E5 the same 20. Shared openings

Later the messages stop matching symbol for symbol and match in pattern instead:

Wherever the first row repeats a symbol, the second row repeats one too, five cells later and with different symbols. Two stretches like this are isomorphs, or copies; the five is their shift. There are 30 such pairs.

And no symbol is ever next to itself: there are no doubles in 1,018 adjacent pairs.

A deck of cards explains the copies

A cipher that replaced each letter by a fixed symbol would turn equal text into equal symbols. The copies have equal patterns and different symbols, so the key changes as the message goes along. The picture used on these pages is a deck of 83 cards numbered 0 to 82. Each letter of the text has its own position in the deck. To encipher a letter, write down the card at its position, then shuffle the deck in a fixed way that depends on the letter. The same text enciphered from a differently ordered deck then comes out with the same pattern of repeats in different symbols. The model, with a worked example on nine cards

Nobody knows the text, so "these two stretches are the same text" is always an assumption. It comes in two strengths, named R and W on every page:

RTwo copies hold the same letter at the cells where their symbols repeat (the lettered cells above).
WTwo copies hold the same letters at every cell.

One shuffle per letter is impossible

The simplest deck cipher has one shuffle and applies it after every letter. Three pairs of copies rule it out:

If the deck were shuffled the same way after every letter, ten shuffles would take 68 to 41. One shuffle takes 68 to 30 and nine more take 30 to 26. Each of the three mappings is seen at two cells, so the argument needs equal letters only where symbols repeat. The cells and the certificate

A link is a symbol in one copy paired with the symbol at the same place in the other. Of 451 links, 114 are seen at two or more cells. The one-shuffle contradiction uses only those. The proof that two copy maps do not commute, the six-symbol change where E4 and E5 break, and the exclusion of affine shuffles all use cells seen once. Zero doubles: 1 in 20, not 1 in 230,000

No symbol is followed by itself in 1,018 adjacent pairs. If the pairs were independent draws from 83 symbols, that would happen once in 230,000 tries. They are not independent: with a fixed key, a given pair of letters either always makes a double or never does. In 400 random class-shuffle keys applied to English text, 4.8% produced no doubles. The condition and the experiment

A trillion seeded keys, no hit

If the key was made by a program, it came from a random number generator started from a seed, most often the clock. The eyes first shipped between the builds of 15 July and 15 October 2020. Every second of that window was tried as a seed for the MSVC, glibc and LuaJIT generators, across thousands of ways to lay out a key each. A candidate key is scored by how many cells it decrypts before it fails: the best reaches 44 of 918, and a true key reaches all 918 (the 1,027 cells after cell 0, with shared openings counted once). What was searched and what was not

The text was probably written out more than once

E1, W1 and E2 each contain one passage twice, 28, 30 and 35 cells apart, after a shared opening and a short stretch that differs. Verse and prose laid out in reading order rarely give several messages that open alike; a record written out three times with different numbers always did, and a song with a chorus often did when each message started at a verse. What is not known

The key, the alphabet, the language, and whether the text has spaces. The class-shuffle model reproduces the shared openings, the isomorphs and the way messages agree again after differing, and no key for it has been found. A wrong guess of the text can pass every quick check: 3 of 120 did in one test. A solution is a key that re-encrypts the text to all nine messages.
