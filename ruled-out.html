Exclusions & searches

Ciphers that cannot have produced the eyes, and keys that were tried. Most rows are variations on the deck picture: a deck of 83 cards, a letter reads the card at its position, and the deck then changes. The exclusions are grouped by what each argument assumes about the plaintext: nothing, R, or W. An isomorph is a pair of stretches with the same pattern of repeated symbols. R says the two stretches hold equal letters at the cells whose symbols repeat; W says they do at every cell. A is the size of the alphabet, the number of different characters in the plaintext.

Needs R

MechanismWhy it fails
One substitution shared by all messagesEqual letters would give equal symbols. The repeat-supported cells of the E1/E2 example hold different symbols.
One shuffle after every letterTen steps would take 68 to both 41 and 26. Also needs fixed read positions and one starting deck for all messages. Certificate.
Steps that depend on which card is where (Solitaire, Chaocipher, RC4-style swaps)The same text enciphered from two different states gives two different repeat patterns, so the isomorphs would be coincidences. An RC4-style deck produced no isomorphs in simulation. Lymm's wiki calls the property these ciphers lack perfect isomorphism.

Needs W

MechanismWhy it fails
Copy maps that are rotations of one ring (cyclic deck walks, additive autokeys)Rotations commute. Two maps from one E3 passage give 54 in one order and 10 in the other. Worked example.
Affine shuffles, card x to ax + b mod 83: all that cuts, reversals and faro shuffles can produceThe three copies that start at E4 68, W4 71 and E5 69 allow two affine solutions, and neither extends to any of the other nine copy maps tested.
Dihedral shuffles: rotate or reflect the deckNone of the 4,096 ways to choose rotation or reflection for the twelve maps fits. Eleven of the twelve are not their own inverse. Lymm's wiki has the same exclusion.
Homophonic substitution: several symbols per letter, each symbol always the same letterCopies of the same text must map each symbol to another symbol of the same letter. Merging the copy maps puts all 82 symbols that occur in copies into one group, so they would all be one letter. Holds with any one pair dropped.

A copy map is the rule that turns each symbol of one copy into the symbol at the same place in the other. The affine and dihedral rows test the twelve copy maps with four or more repeats. Three of them, between E4, W4 and E5, come from one passage and are fitted first; the other nine are then tried one at a time.

The affine exclusion is where R and W part. Using only repeat-supported cells, none of the 1,681 multiplier pairs of the subgroup tested (C83:C41, the half of the affine group whose multipliers are squares) is rejected: the check finds no two symbols forced onto one card, so R alone does not exclude affine shuffles. That is weaker than finding a fitting key. allthemalware's noiteyes reports the affine model as inconclusive once the ends of each isomorph are cut back; FrobtheBuilder excludes it on the full spans. The difference is this assumption. The link counts show how much of the evidence sits on cells that only W uses.

Cell 0 as a key that sets up the deck also fails, on a third assumption: all nine messages agree at cells 1 and 2 after nine different values of cell 0. If the shared openings are shared text, cell 0 does not set the deck.

Reversal and block transposition

If the output were written backwards, shared plaintext openings would appear as shared endings. No two messages share their last symbol. Enciphering reversed plaintext is not tested by this.

A transposition applied after encryption, permuting cells within blocks of 2 to 8, was checked exhaustively: 362,843 combinations of permutation and phase. None keeps the five places where the shared openings split and the isomorphs at a shift of one. Recorded counts.

Key searches

A key for the class-shuffle model is a starting deck and a few shuffles. If a program made them, it most likely seeded a random number generator and shuffled the numbers 0 to 82 several times. One seed gives one key, so every seed can be tried.

The build of 15 July 2020 has no eyes; the 1.0 build of 15 October 2020 has them. The game's executable seeds C rand from the clock and shuffles with std::random_shuffle. MSVC and glibc are the Windows and Linux C libraries, whose rand differ; LuaJIT runs the game's Lua scripts and has its own generator. The clock searches below try every second of that window as a seed. Each seed is tried with a few thousand variants, the ways to turn the shuffles into a key: 25 or 26 letters and ten digits, with or without a space, one to three classes, the deck generated before or after the shuffles. The first runs start a month earlier, on 15 June.

A candidate key decrypts each message until it reaches a symbol it cannot produce. The score is the total length decrypted, out of 918 (shared openings counted once). A true key scores 918. Each of the clock, Lua and card-trick searches was first run on eight sets of test messages enciphered with a known key, and found all eight keys. In the clock test runs the best wrong keys scored 33 to 36.

The encoder might have avoided doubles by shuffling again whenever one was about to appear. Such a key still decrypts correctly up to its first such place, so most of those encoders are covered too: an estimated 75–80%. Outside the searches: other seed sources (a world seed, a fixed constant above a million), other alphabet orders, std::shuffle with mt19937, hand-written shuffle loops, and any key built by hand.
