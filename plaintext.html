Plaintext

What the copies say about the text. A copy is one of two stretches of ciphertext with the same pattern of repeated symbols (an isomorph). Everything here is read through R: where the repeats of two copies line up, the text is the same. Language, alphabet and spacing are unknown, and nothing here decides them.

The second copy comes 28 cells after the first in E1, 30 in W1 and 35 in E2. These pages call the repeated passage the phrase.

Across messages, W1 30–51 is isomorphic to E2 five cells later, and W1 64–81 to E2 ten cells later. So E2 has five more characters than W1 before the phrase and five more between the two copies. The three messages read as one layout: the 24-cell shared opening, a short part that varies, a phrase of about 24 characters, another short part of varying width, the phrase again, a tail.

In running text, how often does a 22-character stretch come back 30 or 35 characters later? In the game's own text, 10 to 12 times in each language, mostly in lists of material and spell names. In the English Kalevala, 5 times, in parallel verse lines. In five hermetic and alchemical books of 200,000 to 500,000 characters each, 0 to 3 times, in chapter headings, contents lists and the Project Gutenberg licence. Lists and filled-in templates have this shape; prose does not.

E1 and W1 differ at cells 25–28 and 33–36 and agree before, between and after. E4 and E5 differ at 21–24 and agree again at 25–27. A field is a short stretch where otherwise matching messages differ, like a blank filled in differently on each copy of a form. Of the 58 stretches left free by the constraint sheet, 13 are exactly four cells long. Under the class-shuffle model, the simplest way for the output to agree again after a difference is that the letters that differed belong to one class. Four-character fields of one class look like numbers. That reading is open.

The eyes as a fill-in sheet

Tie together the cells that must hold the same letter and the 1,027 body cells (every cell but cell 0 of each message) become fewer letters to choose. Each row adds a set of ties to the rows above it:

The last row contradicts itself. E1 and W1 are each isomorphic to E2 five cells later, so W ties E1 33–36 to W1 33–36 through E2 38–41, and those cells hold different symbols. With matching decks, which the agreement at 29–32 suggests, different symbols are different letters. The four cells hold symbols seen nowhere else in either passage. So an isomorph can span a short field where the text differs without showing it, and W cannot hold for every cell of every listed pair. W2, E3 and W3 are barely tied at all: runs of 70, 48 and 87 cells with no constraint. The sheet.

A candidate was rejected if it broke the pattern of the ciphertext: different text where the messages share an opening, or different letters where an isomorph repeats. Generated records were screened against the constraint sheet and the closure check instead.

The two posted solutions give E1, W1 and E2 different first lines; those messages share their first 24 symbols. A class split is a guess at which letters share a shuffle; the two main ones are vowels, consonants and digits, and letters, digits and space. Of the generated records, 38 pass when every letter may have its own shuffle and 3 under a third class split; a search for a full key ran out of time on each of them. This excludes those strings at those alignments. It does not exclude the Kalevala or the game's vocabulary as a source.

How much text a check needs

On small decks of 11 to 23 cards, wrong guesses shorter than about 30–36 symbols still have a key that fits; longer ones are refuted. Scaled to 83 cards the crossover is about 220–230 symbols, or 270–300 if the card just read also moves. That is an extrapolation and good to an order of magnitude. The eyes have 1,036 symbols, so there is enough ciphertext in principle; a guess covering one message is too short to confirm.

The instant closure check is much weaker than a full key search. It takes about 0.1 seconds per guess and removes guesses that contradict themselves, and a guess that survives still needs a key.
